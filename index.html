Bayes Lens

One theorem, three worlds: what a positive medical test, an AI's cancer flag, and your social media feed each reveal once you apply Bayes' rule.

Probability and statistics project by Youness

A positive test is not a diagnosis

A test's accuracy describes how it behaves when we already know who is sick. A patient needs the reverse: given this result, how likely is it that I am sick? Bayes' rule converts one into the other, and the answer depends heavily on how common the disease was before anyone was tested.

Following the probabilities

The tree applies the product rule on each path, P(D ∩ +) = P(D) × P(+ | D), then Bayes' rule keeps only the positive leaves: P(D | +) = P(D ∩ +) / P(+).

Testing again: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior

After one result, the posterior becomes the prior for the next test. Click a result to flip it between positive and negative. This chain assumes each test is independent given the disease status, which is the same assumption a Naive Bayes classifier makes about its features.

Chance that a test just repeats the previous result for a reason unrelated to the disease, such as the same benign lesion or the same lab error. With ρ = 0 the tests are independent.

Limitations this tab exposes

  • The prior dominates. The same test gives very different posteriors in a screening population and in a clinic of symptomatic patients. A single "accuracy" number cannot tell a patient what a positive means.
  • Sensitivity and specificity are estimates. They come from a study sample, carry sampling error, and often shift when the test is used on a different population (spectrum bias).
  • Repeat tests are rarely independent. Multiplying likelihoods as if they were independent overstates certainty when errors share a cause, as the shared-error setting shows.
  • Rounded people. The grid and tree use rounded expected counts; the exact posterior always comes from the formula.

When an AI reads the scan

Imaging AI is sold on sensitivity, specificity and accuracy. Those are likelihoods: they describe the AI when we already know who has cancer. Whether a woman the AI flags actually has cancer is a posterior, and Bayes' rule says it depends on how rare cancer is where the AI is used. Part A checks this against two published mammography AI systems. Part B builds a Bayesian classifier from real imaging data and shows where the prior hides inside a trained model.

Part B. Train a Naive Bayes classifier on real imaging data

The Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset (Street, Wolberg and Mangasarian, 1993; UCI Machine Learning Repository) describes 569 breast masses. Each row holds measurements of cell nuclei computed from a digitized microscope image of a fine-needle aspirate, and a biopsy-confirmed label: 212 malignant, 357 benign. The app splits it into 398 training and 171 test patients (stratified, fixed seed), then trains a Naive Bayes classifier in your browser by counting.

Naive Bayes applies Bayes' rule with many pieces of evidence at once. It assumes the features are independent given the class, so the likelihood of a patient's whole profile is a product: P(features | M) = P(x₁ | M) × P(x₂ | M) × … The same assumption drove the repeat-test chain in the Diagnosis tab.

Limitations this tab exposes

  • "Cancer" has to be defined before sensitivity means anything. Google's system scores 54.1% sensitivity when cancers that appear up to 39 months later count, and 91.3% (with 94.1% specificity) when only cancers found at that screening count. Same model, same images.
  • A threshold is a choice. The AI outputs a score; someone picks the cut-off that turns it into a flag. The Danish study used a cut-off that matched the first radiologist's sensitivity. A different cut-off gives a different sensitivity and specificity pair.
  • A score is not a probability. The UK authors report their model was not perfectly calibrated to produce a disease probability. Only Bayes' rule with the right prior turns the output into one.
  • Populations drift. In the UK deployment, a newer mammography machine produced a recall rate of 10.9% against 6.3% on the older model, and the threshold had to be recalibrated. The likelihoods themselves moved.
  • Verification bias. In the Danish study, women the AI flagged but radiologists did not recall never got a work-up, so some AI "false positives" may be unconfirmed cancers.
  • The Wisconsin data is small and enriched. 569 patients from one institution, cytology rather than mammography, 37% malignant. Binning throws away detail, and the independence assumption is visibly false for radius, perimeter and area.

A feed that learns what hooks you

A feed has one question to answer before every post: which kind of content is this person most likely to engage with? It cannot know, so it keeps a belief for each content type and updates it with Bayes' rule after every swipe. Recommender teams use this family of methods in production: Netflix's engineers have described contextual bandits with Thompson sampling and other Bayesian methods for choosing what to show each member. This tab runs a simplified version on a simulated user, or on you.

How the feed thinks

The belief. For each content type k, the unknown engagement rate is θk = P(engage | type k). Before any data, every value from 0 to 1 is equally plausible: a uniform prior.

The update. After s engagements in n views, Bayes' rule multiplies that prior by the likelihood θs(1 − θ)n − s. The mean of the resulting posterior is (s + 1) / (n + 2), known as Laplace's rule of succession. It is the same "add one" idea as Laplace smoothing in the Imaging AI tab.

The choice. Thompson sampling draws one plausible θ for each type from its posterior and shows the type with the highest draw. A type the feed is unsure about sometimes wins by luck. That is exploration, and it is what lets the feed discover new hooks.

Why this is the addiction question

Two learners are running at once. The feed learns which content type pays off for this user and serves more of it. The user learns that scrolling sometimes pays off, but never predictably: even the favourite type hooks only some of the time. Reward that arrives on an unpredictable schedule is the pattern behavioural psychology links to the most persistent habits, and Lindström and colleagues found that posting behaviour on real platforms fits reward-learning models, based on over one million posts from more than 4,000 people.

Nothing in the algorithm is malicious. It maximises one number, the probability of engagement, and Bayes' rule makes it very good at that. Whether the user is better off is not in the objective. The guardrail setting shows that a design choice, not the mathematics, decides how narrow the feed becomes.

Limitations this tab exposes

  • Real feeds are far richer. Production systems condition on context such as time of day, device, history and the post itself, and blend many models. Platforms rarely publish their exact method, so this is a model of the idea, not of any specific app.
  • Engagement is a proxy. A click or a long view is not enjoyment, and certainly not wellbeing. The feed optimises what it can measure.
  • The simulated user never changes. Real preferences drift, and the feed itself shapes them, a feedback loop this model leaves out.
  • Each view is treated as an independent coin flip given the content type. Real engagement depends on mood, fatigue and the previous post.
  • The prior matters early. With a uniform prior and few views, beliefs are wide and the first lucky engagements steer the feed. This is the cold-start problem.
  • No causal claim. The simulation shows how an engagement-maximising Bayesian feed behaves. It does not prove that any real platform causes addiction.

Where Bayes runs in real AI, and where it breaks

The classic application: a Bayesian spam filter

In 2002 Paul Graham's essay "A Plan for Spam" replaced hand-written spam rules with statistics: count how often each word appears in spam and in normal mail, then combine the words with Bayes' rule. Treating the words as independent makes it a Naive Bayes classifier, the same model as in the Imaging AI tab. Click words to put them in the email.

Graham's formula quietly assumes 50%. Change it and watch the verdict move.

Word probabilities here are illustrative, chosen to look like typical values. A real filter estimates each one by counting words in thousands of labelled emails.

How this tool was built with AI, and how it was checked

The code for this app was generated by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) in a step-by-step conversation on claude.ai. Each tab was specified, generated, tested and approved before the next one was started. Published numbers were first looked up in the original papers, not taken from the AI's memory.

An AI code generator fails in predictable ways: it can produce plausible but wrong numbers, cite sources that do not exist, and write code that runs but computes the wrong thing. The checks below target each of those. They run live in your browser against values computed separately in Python with NumPy and scikit-learn.

Problems caught during testing: a quote-escaping error that would have stopped the whole Imaging AI tab from loading, a singular or plural wording bug ("1 are sick"), and a note that became wrong after a new metric was added. Each was found by running the page headlessly and reading its output, not by trusting the generated code.

What the checks cannot prove: that the chosen models are the right ones for a real hospital or platform, or that the illustrative spam probabilities match any real corpus. Those are judgement calls, discussed in the limitations above.
